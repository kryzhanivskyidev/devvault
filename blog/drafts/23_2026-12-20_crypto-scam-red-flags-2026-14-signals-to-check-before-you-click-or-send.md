# Crypto Scam Red Flags 2026: 14 Signals to Check Before You Click or Send

Crypto scams no longer look like badly written emails. They arrive as realistic exchange support chats, cloned login pages, investment groups with manufactured profits, sponsored search results, AI-generated video calls, and wallet prompts that hide broad permissions.

The most useful defense is a short list of behaviours that end the interaction immediately. These fourteen signals apply whether the scam impersonates WhiteBIT, a hardware-wallet brand, an influencer, or a recovery specialist.

> **Quick verdict:** No legitimate exchange, wallet provider, or support agent needs your seed phrase, private key, password, or remote control of your device.
>
> **Best for:** every crypto user, especially people joining communities, seeking support, or moving funds under time pressure.
>
> **Look elsewhere if:** anyone hoping a single scam checker can replace independent verification.

## Red flags 1–4: money promises

1. **Guaranteed returns:** No legitimate crypto product can guarantee high profit without risk.
2. **Urgent deposit window:** Scarcity is used to stop research.
3. **Pay more to unlock withdrawals:** Taxes or fees demanded in new crypto transfers are a classic advance-fee pattern.
4. **Profits visible only on one dashboard:** A number on a website is not evidence that funds exist or can leave.

## Red flags 5–8: identity and support

5. **Unsolicited support message:** Real support rarely starts in a direct message.
6. **Seed phrase or private-key request:** Sharing either gives control of the wallet.
7. **Remote-access request:** Screen-sharing or remote desktop can expose passwords, files, and signing prompts.
8. **Conversation moved to a private channel:** Scammers isolate victims from public warnings and official records.

## Red flags 9–11: links and addresses

9. **Look-alike domain:** Extra characters, unusual subdomains, or a sponsored result can lead to a cloned site.
10. **Address supplied in chat:** Generate or verify addresses inside the official destination.
11. **A familiar-looking address in transaction history:** Address-poisoning transactions use matching prefixes and suffixes. Verify the full intended source.

## Red flags 12–14: wallet permissions

12. **Blind-signing pressure:** A prompt you cannot understand should not be approved.
13. **Unlimited token approval for a trivial task:** Broad allowances can let a contract drain future balances.
14. **Recovery specialist demanding upfront crypto:** Victims are often targeted again by people promising to recover lost funds.

## The five-minute verification routine

Close the message. Open the provider from a saved bookmark. Check official status and support channels. Search the exact domain or wallet contract through an authoritative source. Ask a trusted person to review the situation without sharing secrets.

If money must move immediately, that is a reason to wait. Legitimate security processes can explain the threat without asking you to transfer funds to a “safe wallet.”

## If you already clicked or signed

Disconnect the wallet from the site, review and revoke token approvals, move remaining assets from a clean device if the seed may be exposed, change compromised passwords, revoke exchange sessions and API keys, and contact official support.

Preserve transaction IDs, domains, messages, and timestamps for reporting. Do not pay a stranger to reverse an irreversible transaction.

## FAQ

### Can a VPN stop crypto scams?

No. A VPN protects network transport; it does not verify a website, token contract, or support identity.

### Will WhiteBIT support ask for my seed phrase?

No legitimate exchange support needs a self-custody seed phrase or private key.
